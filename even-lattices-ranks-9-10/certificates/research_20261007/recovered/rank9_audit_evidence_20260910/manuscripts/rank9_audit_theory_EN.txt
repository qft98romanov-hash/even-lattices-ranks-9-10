\textbf{Status and scope.} This document explains the computer-assisted
result \(e_9(6)=21870\), audits the mathematical reduction and its exact
certificates, and distinguishes certificate validation from exhaustive

\begin{abstract}
We study positive-definite even integral lattices of rank nine and minimum
at least six, where ``minimum'' always means squared Euclidean length. The
claimed exact optimum is determinant \(21870\). An explicit lattice
attains this value. To prove the lower bound, a hypothetical counterexample
is cut repeatedly by shortest primitive dual vectors. Hermite bounds,
Mordell's inequality, determinant congruences, and a lifting inequality
place this saturated flag in finite determinant windows. Complete exact
one-vector extension enumeration reaches \(2235\) labelled rank-seven
forms; \(96\) survive the lifting bound and are identified with six
representatives. Exact discriminant-class minima close their possible
rank-eight continuations.

The exposition supplies the linear algebra, lattice theory, arithmetic
algorithms, completeness arguments, matrices, and numerical derivations
needed to understand this proof. It also corrects the scaling notation for
the determinant-\(8192\) lattice, makes the parent-minimum hypothesis in the
layer criterion explicit, and identifies an omitted zero-residue layer in
one generic helper. That helper defect does not change the particular
branch conclusions, for a proved numerical reason. The result determines
an abstract rank-nine determinant minimum and its consequence for the
bare dimension-39 cross-section construction. Uniqueness, full
classification, rank ten, and cluster optimality remain separate questions.
\end{abstract}

\section{The original problem and the exact claim being audited}
\label{sec:question}

The source is the supplied confidential manuscript by I.~Dorofeev,
X.~Sun, and C.~Wang, \emph{Optimal Extensions of Cross-Sections: Sphere
Packings in Dimensions 38 to 43}. The relevant passage in Section~7,
page~13 reads as follows. Only line breaks and mathematical typesetting
have been changed.

\begin{quotation}\small
Table 4 localizes what is left for the bare cross-section route and
separates unconditional ceilings from current-record benchmarks. In
dimensions 40, 41 and 42 the bare route is closed: the sections of
Corollary 11 are optimal among bare cross-section packings. This does not
rule out antipode clusters over these or other sections. In dimension 43
the bare route is also closed, while the cluster route is essential for
our improvement and may admit further gains.

The interesting bare cases are dimensions 39 and 38. In dimension 39, any
rank-9 section with determinant below 21870 would improve our present
packing. The current published rank-9 lattice record corresponds to the
benchmark 19683: under the published-literature convention used here, a
determinant below that is not excluded by an optimality theorem, but would
simultaneously produce a new 9-dimensional lattice record. Thus
\([19683,21870)\) is the portion of the search interval that could improve
dimension 39 without necessarily improving that lower-dimensional record.
If the recent dimension-9 preprint cited above is correct, then
determinants below 19683 are excluded and this interval is the full
rigorous search window. Likewise, for dimension 38 one seeks determinant
below 54675. The current rank-10 lattice record gives the exact benchmark
\(3^{11}/4=44286.75\); because section determinants are integral,
\(\det K\le44286\) would also improve the rank-10 lattice record, while
\(44287\le\det K<54675\) could improve dimension 38 without doing so.

Any such section inside an even ambient lattice must itself be an even
integral lattice of minimum at least 6, which is a real constraint: at the
rank-9 benchmark \(19683=3^9\) the natural candidate, the rescaled
laminated lattice \(\sqrt{\tfrac32}\,\Lambda_9\), is not integral, so a rank-9
section of that determinant would have to be some other lattice. We do not
know which even integral lattices of minimum 6 occur in ranks 9 and 10, and
determining their smallest determinant would sharpen these searches. The
current lattice records alone do not provide rigorous determinant floors
in these ranks; the recent dimension-9 preprint, if correct, supplies the
rank-9 floor. We leave the corresponding integral-section questions open.
\end{quotation}

The statements about the literature in this quotation are part of the
historical source text. The proof developed below does not use the
dimension-nine preprint mentioned there. Its rank-nine Hermite estimate is
the unconditional bound derived from dimension eight by Mordell's
inequality.

\begin{definition}
For a positive integer \(r\), put
\[
e_r(6)=\inf\{\det L: L\text{ is positive definite, even and integral},
\ \rk L=r,\ \min L\ge6\}.
\]
The set is nonempty, since \(\sqrt6\Z^r\) belongs to it. Its determinants
are positive integers, so the infimum is a minimum.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[the audited rank-nine statement]
\label{thm:main}
Every positive-definite even integral lattice \(L\) of rank nine with
\(\min L\ge6\) satisfies \(\det L\ge21870\). The explicit Gram matrix in
Section~\ref{sec:k9} attains this bound. Consequently
\[
\boxed{e_9(6)=21870.}
\]
The lower-bound proof uses the complete finite computations specified
below, together with the stated classical Hermite theorem in ranks at
most eight.
\end{theorem}

This quantifies over \emph{all abstract} even integral lattices of rank
nine. It therefore gives a necessary lower bound for every rank-nine
section inside an even ambient lattice of minimum six. A converse statement
that every such abstract lattice embeds in a particular ambient lattice
would be a different assertion and is not used.

The theorem closes the rank-nine minimum-determinant part of the quoted
question. It does not give a list of all rank-nine lattices, prove that the
minimizer is unique, or settle the rank-ten question in the same passage.

\section{Lattices, Gram matrices, and normalization}

\subsection{Why the determinant is the square of the covolume}
Let \(V\) be an \(r\)-dimensional Euclidean space and let
\(b_1,\ldots,b_r\) be a real basis. A lattice is the discrete subgroup
\[
L=\Z b_1+\cdots+\Z b_r.
\]
In an orthonormal coordinate system, write \(B\) for the matrix whose
columns are the \(b_i\). The Gram matrix is
\[
G=B^{\mathsf T}B,\qquad G_{ij}=\langle b_i,b_j\rangle.
\]
For an integer coordinate column \(x\), the associated lattice vector is
\(Bx\), and
\[
\|Bx\|^2=x^{\mathsf T}Gx.
\]
The volume of a fundamental parallelepiped is
\(\covol L=|\det B|\). Thus
\[
\det L:=\det G=(\covol L)^2.
\]
This convention matters: a bound on the Gram determinant is the square of
the corresponding covolume bound.

Another lattice basis has the form \(BX\), where
\(X\in\GL_r(\Z)\), equivalently \(X\) is integral and \(\det X=\pm1\).
The new Gram matrix is \(X^{\mathsf T}GX\). Its determinant is unchanged.
Two Gram matrices \(A,R\) define integrally isometric lattices precisely
when an integer matrix \(X\) satisfies
\[
X^{\mathsf T}RX=A,\qquad |\det X|=1.
\]
The equality alone, without unimodularity or a same-determinant argument,
can describe a proper sublattice and is not generally an isometry
certificate.

\subsection{Minimum, integrality, evenness, and scaling}
The minimum is
\[
\min L=\min_{0\ne x\in\Z^r}x^{\mathsf T}Gx.
\]
Throughout this note it is a \emph{squared} length. Positive definiteness
implies that a bounded ball meets the lattice in finitely many points, so
this positive minimum is attained.

The lattice is integral if \(G\) has integer entries. It is even if,
in addition, its diagonal entries are even. Indeed,
\[
x^{\mathsf T}Gx=\sum_iG_{ii}x_i^2+2\sum_{i<j}G_{ij}x_ix_j.
\]
Thus even diagonal entries suffice, and taking \(x=e_i\) proves their
necessity. A positive-definite even integral lattice has positive norms in
\(2\Z\); therefore proving \(\min L\ge6\) amounts to excluding norms
\(2\) and \(4\). An exact enumeration through norm \(5\) is a convenient
single check.

If all vectors are multiplied by \(c>0\), then
\[
G(cL)=c^2G(L),\quad \min(cL)=c^2\min L,\quad
\det(cL)=c^{2r}\det L.
\]
For example, \(\sqrt3E_7\) has Gram matrix three times a root Gram matrix
of \(E_7\). If \(C\) is such a root Gram matrix, \(4C^{-1}\) is the Gram
matrix of \(2E_7^*\), not of \(4E_7^*\). The latter would have Gram
matrix \(16C^{-1}\). This resolves a notation error in the earlier draft
and in a legacy transcript; the actual integer matrix used in the
computation is unchanged.

\subsection{Indices and saturated sublattices}
If \(J\subset L\) has the same rank and index \(h\), a basis matrix of
\(J\) is \(BX\) with \(|\det X|=h\). Hence
\begin{equation}
\det J=h^2\det L.
\label{eq:index}
\end{equation}
For a lower-rank sublattice \(J\subset L\), its saturation is
\[
J^{\mathrm{sat}}=L\cap\Span_{\Q}J.
\]
The following conditions are equivalent: \(J=J^{\mathrm{sat}}\);
\(L/J\) is torsion-free; and a basis of \(J\) extends to a basis of
\(L\). The equivalence follows from Smith normal form: the torsion
invariant factors in a presentation of the inclusion must all equal one.
A sublattice with these properties is called saturated or primitive.

Saturation is essential in the proof. If \(J\) has corank one and is
saturated, then \(L/J\cong\Z\), so one vector extends a basis of \(J\)
to a basis of \(L\). Without saturation, a one-vector extension does not
describe the whole lattice and a determinant computation may miss an
index-square factor.

\section{Duality, discriminant groups, and class minima}
\label{sec:discriminant}

\subsection{The dual lattice in coordinates}
The dual is
\[
L^*=\{y\in V:\langle y,L\rangle\subseteq\Z\}.
\]
Writing \(y=B\xi\), its pairing column with the lattice basis is
\(p=G\xi\). Thus
\[
y\in L^*\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p\in\Z^r,
\qquad \xi=G^{-1}p,
\qquad \|y\|^2=p^{\mathsf T}G^{-1}p.
\]
The dual Gram matrix in the dual basis is \(G^{-1}\), and
\(\det L^*=1/\det L\). When \(L\) is integral, \(L\subseteq L^*\).
The quotient
\[
A_L=L^*/L\cong\Z^r/G\Z^r
\]
is the discriminant group. Its order is \(\det G\).

More explicitly, if \(UGV=\diag(s_1,\ldots,s_r)\) is a Smith normal
form, where \(U,V\) are unimodular and \(s_i\mid s_{i+1}\), then
\[
A_L\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^r\Z/s_i\Z,
\qquad |A_L|=\prod_i s_i=\det G.
\]
Smith normal form explains the finite group; the actual verification code
can avoid computing it by using fractional-coordinate fingerprints.

\subsection{The finite quadratic form and what it does not record}
For an even integral lattice, the functions
\[
b_L(a,b)=\langle y,z\rangle\bmod\Z,
\qquad q_L(a)=\|y\|^2\bmod2\Z
\]
are well defined for \(a=y+L\), \(b=z+L\). To verify the second claim,
replace \(y\) by \(y+x\), \(x\in L\). The norm changes by
\(2\langle y,x\rangle+\|x\|^2\in2\Z\). Also
\[
q_L(a+b)-q_L(a)-q_L(b)=2b_L(a,b)\pmod{2\Z}.
\]
This finite quadratic form controls the parity condition in an even
extension. It does not determine the smallest Euclidean norm in a class:
the residue of a number modulo \(2\Z\) loses its integer part.

Define instead
\[
\mu_L(a)=\min_{y\in a}\|y\|^2,
\qquad \rhog(L)^2=\max_{a\in A_L}\mu_L(a).
\]
The zero class has \(\mu_L(0)=0\), and
\(\mu_L(-a)=\mu_L(a)\). The number \(\rhog(L)\) is a
\emph{discrete glue radius}. It is the largest distance from a dual
lattice point to \(L\). It must not be confused with the ordinary
covering radius
\[
R(L)=\sup_{v\in V}\dist(v,L),
\qquad \rhog(L)\le R(L).
\]
Equality is not assumed. The exact quantity needed in the proof is the
maximum over the finite group \(A_L\).

\subsection{Exact fingerprints and a completeness certificate}
Let \(d=\det G\), \(A=\adj G=dG^{-1}\). Both \(d\) and \(A\) are
integral. Associate to a pairing column \(p\in\Z^r\) the fingerprint
\begin{equation}
\kappa(p)=Ap\pmod d\in(\Z/d\Z)^r.
\label{eq:fingerprint}
\end{equation}
Then
\[
\kappa(p)=\kappa(q)
\Longleftrightarrow G^{-1}(p-q)\in\Z^r
\Longleftrightarrow p-q\in G\Z^r.
\]
Consequently fingerprints distinguish precisely the discriminant classes,
and there are exactly \(d\) possible fingerprints.

Enumerate every integer \(p\) satisfying
\(p^{\mathsf T}Ap\le dB\), for a rational bound \(B\), and record the
smallest value \(p^{\mathsf T}Ap/d\) for each fingerprint. If all \(d\)
fingerprints occur, these recorded numbers are the \emph{exact} class
minima. Indeed, each class has a representative of norm at most \(B\),
and every vector of smaller norm was included in the exhaustive ball.
This is more than finding one short representative per class: the
exhaustiveness of the ball is part of the argument.

The program either fixes a bound that covers all classes and verifies the
count \(d\), or increases an integral bound until that count is reached.
For an arbitrary positive-definite lattice this procedure terminates,
because there are finitely many classes, each having a finite minimum.
The implementation's emergency bound is a failure guard: reaching it
raises an error instead of silently declaring an incomplete table complete.

\section{One-vector extensions and the full layer criterion}
\label{sec:extension}

\subsection{The Schur complement as perpendicular squared length}
Suppose \(J\subset K\) is a saturated corank-one sublattice and
\(G\) is a Gram matrix of \(J\). Choose \(v\in K\) whose image
generates \(K/J\). Then a Gram matrix of \(K\) is
\begin{equation}
\Ext{G}{p}{n}:=
\begin{pmatrix}G&p\\p^{\mathsf T}&n\end{pmatrix},
\qquad p_i=\langle b_i,v\rangle.
\label{eq:border}
\end{equation}
Write \(v=y+z\), where \(y\in\Span_{\R}J\), \(z\perp J\).
Integrality implies \(p\in\Z^r\), so \(y\in J^*\), with coordinates
\(G^{-1}p\). Put
\[
s=\|z\|^2=n-p^{\mathsf T}G^{-1}p.
\]
Block elimination gives
\begin{equation}
\det K=(\det J)s,
\qquad K>0\ \Longleftrightarrow\ G>0\text{ and }s>0.
\label{eq:schur}
\end{equation}
Here \(K>0\) denotes positive definiteness. The identity is the algebraic
expression of ``base volume times height'' for a parallelepiped.

Replacing \(v\) by \(v+x\), \(x\in J\), is a unimodular change of
basis. It changes \(y\) within its class \(a\in A_J\), while \(s\)
is unchanged. We can therefore choose \(y\) to be a minimum-norm
representative, with \(\|y\|^2=\mu_J(a)\). The last diagonal entry is
then \(n=s+\mu_J(a)\).

\begin{proposition}[complete extension criterion]
\label{prop:layers}
Assume that \(J\) is positive definite, even and integral, and
\(\min J\ge6\). A choice of \(a\in A_J\) and \(s\in\Q_{>0}\)
defines a positive-definite corank-one lattice extension. It is integral
if and only if \(s+\mu_J(a)\in\Z\). It is even integral with minimum
at least six if and only if
\begin{align}
s+q_J(a)&=0\quad\text{in }\Q/2\Z,\label{eq:parity}\\
m^2s+\mu_J(ma)&\ge6\quad(m\in\Z\setminus\{0\}).
\label{eq:layers}
\end{align}
Its determinant is \((\det J)s\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Choose \(y\in a\) and a new perpendicular vector \(z\) of squared
length \(s\). Set \(K=J+\Z(y+z)\). Pairings between \(y+z\) and
\(J\) are integral. Evenness requires its norm to belong to \(2\Z\),
which is exactly \eqref{eq:parity}. For any integer \(m\), the layer
\(J+m(y+z)\) has fixed perpendicular component \(mz\). Its least
squared norm is therefore
\[
m^2s+\min_{x\in J}\|x+my\|^2=m^2s+\mu_J(ma).
\]
For \(m=0\), the zero vector must be omitted when testing the lattice
minimum; all remaining vectors already have norm at least six by the
hypothesis on \(J\). For \(m\ne0\), every vector is nonzero and
\eqref{eq:layers} is necessary and sufficient. Formula
\eqref{eq:schur} proves the determinant statement.
\end{proof}

The explicit assumption \(\min J\ge6\) is essential. A layer test for
\(m\ne0\) cannot repair an already short nonzero vector in the parent.
The earlier exposition relied on this hypothesis through the flag context;
the proposition above states it in its own right.

\subsection{Two finite ways of checking all layers}
Because \(\mu_J(ma)\ge0\), only positive integers with
\[
m^2s<6
\]
need testing. Negative \(m\) give the same minima. Thus the largest
necessary positive layer is
\(\lceil\sqrt{6/s}\rceil-1\); a program need not compute this square
root and can simply continue while the exact rational inequality
\(m^2s<6\) holds. Equality \(m^2s=6\) is already safe.

Alternatively, let \(h=\ord(a)\). Testing \(m=1,\ldots,h\) is
sufficient, since for every \(m>h\) its positive residue representative
\(r\in\{1,\ldots,h\}\) satisfies \(ma=ra\) and \(r\le m\).
Symmetry allows a shorter test, but it must include the zero residue:
\begin{equation}
\begin{cases}
m^2s+\mu_J(ma)\ge6,&1\le m\le\lfloor h/2\rfloor,\\
h^2s\ge6.&
\end{cases}
\label{eq:orderlayers}
\end{equation}
For each nonzero residue modulo \(h\), choose its representative with
smallest absolute value; for the zero residue, the shortest \emph{nonzero}
layer index is \(h\), not zero. Omitting the last inequality can falsely
accept an extension containing a short perpendicular vector. The audit of
one legacy helper found exactly this generic omission; its harmlessness
for the actual input is proved in Section~\ref{sec:audit}.

\subsection{Why there are only finitely many candidate extensions}
Fix an allowed determinant interval \(D_-\le\det K\le D_+\), put
\(d=\det J\), and use a shortest representative of class \(a\). Then
\[
\mu_J(a)+\frac{D_-}{d}\le n\le
\mu_J(a)+\frac{D_+}{d},\qquad n\in2\Z.
\]
There are finitely many possible even values of \(n\), and exactly
\(d\) possible classes. For every pair \((a,n)\), the Schur complement,
parity, determinant congruence, and minimum tests are exact rational or
integer tests. This proves finiteness without any unproved bound on the
entries of an arbitrary Gram matrix.

\begin{corollary}[glue-radius lower bound]
\label{cor:glue}
Every positive-definite integral one-vector extension \(K\supset J\)
with minimum at least six satisfies
\begin{equation}
\det K\ge\det J\bigl(6-\rhog(J)^2\bigr).
\label{eq:gluebound}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
The layer \(m=1\) gives \(s\ge6-\mu_J(a)\). Since
\(\mu_J(a)\le\rhog(J)^2\), multiply by \(\det J\).
\end{proof}
This is a necessary bound, not a sufficient construction criterion.
Equality additionally requires a deepest discriminant class, correct
parity, and all other layer inequalities. If the radius square exceeds
six, the bound remains true but need not be useful.

\section{Shortest dual vectors and saturated flags}
\label{sec:flag}

\begin{lemma}[a primitive dual normal]
\label{lem:normal}
Let \(M\) have rank \(r\ge2\), and choose a shortest nonzero
\(y\in M^*\). Then \(y\) is primitive in \(M^*\), and
\(J=M\cap y^\perp\) is a saturated rank-\(r-1\) sublattice. Moreover
\begin{equation}
\det J=(\det M)\|y\|^2=(\det M)\min M^*.
\label{eq:normaldet}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
If \(y=hy_0\), where \(h>1\) is integral and \(y_0\in M^*\), then
\(y_0\) is shorter, a contradiction. In the dual basis, the integer
coordinates of \(y\) therefore have greatest common divisor one. By
B\'ezout's identity the map \(x\mapsto\langle x,y\rangle\) from
\(M\) to \(\Z\) is onto. Its kernel is \(J\), and
\(M/J\cong\Z\), proving rank and saturation.

Choose \(v\in M\) with \(\langle v,y\rangle=1\). Its component
perpendicular to \(J\) is \(y/\|y\|^2\), with square norm
\(1/\|y\|^2\). Apply the Schur complement to the basis \((J,v)\):
\(\det M=\det J/\|y\|^2\).
\end{proof}

Starting from a hypothetical counterexample \(L_9\), repeatedly apply
this lemma to obtain
\begin{equation}
L_1\subset L_2\subset\cdots\subset L_8\subset L_9,
\qquad d_r=\det L_r,
\label{eq:flagchain}
\end{equation}
where every inclusion is saturated, every lattice is even and integral
with minimum at least six, and
\[
\min L_r^*=d_{r-1}/d_r\qquad(2\le r\le9).
\]
All shortest-vector ties are allowed. No canonical choice of a shortest
dual normal is required; at least one such flag exists for every
hypothetical counterexample.

For a proposed step \(J\subset K\) with Schur complement \(s\), the
dual vector orthogonal to \(J\) has norm square \(1/s\). Therefore the
shortest-dual condition is exactly
\begin{equation}
\min K^*=1/s.
\label{eq:shortdual}
\end{equation}
Its verification is finite: enumerate every vector of the dual form
through the rational bound \(1/s\), and check that no nonzero vector
has smaller norm. The normal itself occurs, so this test also has an
explicit witness at the bound.

\section{Hermite bounds and an elementary proof of Mordell's inequality}
\label{sec:hermite}

The Hermite invariant of a rank-\(r\) Euclidean lattice is
\[
\frac{\min L}{(\det L)^{1/r}}.
\]
It is invariant under uniform scaling. The Hermite constant is its
supremum over all lattices, without an integrality condition:
\[
\gamma_r=\sup_{\rk L=r}\frac{\min L}{(\det L)^{1/r}}.
\]
It follows directly that
\begin{equation}
\min L\le\gamma_r(\det L)^{1/r},
\qquad \min L^*\le\gamma_r(\det L)^{-1/r}.
\label{eq:hermite}
\end{equation}
The constants are finite. For completeness, a short route to finiteness
uses the convex-body lattice theorem: a centrally symmetric convex body
of volume greater than \(2^r\covol L\) contains a nonzero lattice
point. To see the underlying argument, a measurable set of volume greater
than one fundamental domain has two distinct points differing by a
lattice vector, by translating its pieces into that domain. Apply this to
half the symmetric body; its difference set lies in the original body by
convexity and symmetry. Taking Euclidean balls gives a finite upper bound
for \(\gamma_r\). No numerical version of that bound enters the search.

\begin{theorem}[classical low-dimensional Hermite theorem; external input]
\label{thm:hermiteexternal}
For \(1\le r\le8\), the exact values are
\[
\begin{array}{c|rrrrrrrr}
r&1&2&3&4&5&6&7&8\\\hline
\gamma_r^r&1&4/3&2&4&8&64/3&64&256.
\end{array}
\]
In dimension eight, equality in the Hermite bound is attained only by
lattices similar to \(E_8\).
\end{theorem}

This is a substantive external theorem, not a fact proved by the finite
programs. The values are tabulated, for example, by Wen and Chang
\cite{WenChang}; the classical lattice sources are
\cite{CS,Martinet,Blichfeldt}. The dimension-eight equality statement is
also a consequence of Viazovska's optimality and uniqueness theorem for
periodic packings \cite{Viazovska}, because a lattice packing is periodic.
We state this dependency explicitly instead of silently turning an
enumeration into a proof of the Hermite constants themselves.

\begin{proposition}[Mordell's inequality]
For \(r\ge3\),
\begin{equation}
\gamma_r\le\gamma_{r-1}^{(r-1)/(r-2)}.
\label{eq:mordell}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Scale any rank-\(r\) lattice \(M\) to determinant one. Choose a
shortest nonzero vector of \(M^*\), and let \(J\) be its primitive
orthogonal section. Lemma~\ref{lem:normal} gives
\(\det J=\min M^*\le\gamma_r\). Since \(J\subset M\),
\[
\min M\le\min J
\le\gamma_{r-1}(\det J)^{1/(r-1)}
\le\gamma_{r-1}\gamma_r^{1/(r-1)}.
\]
Take the supremum over all determinant-one \(M\). Finiteness and
positivity of \(\gamma_r\) justify division and give
\(\gamma_r^{(r-2)/(r-1)}\le\gamma_{r-1}\), which is the claim.
No existence of a lattice attaining the supremum is needed.
\end{proof}

For the rank-nine proof this gives
\[
\gamma_9\le\gamma_8^{8/7}=2^{8/7}.
\]
Applying \eqref{eq:hermite} to each stage of
\eqref{eq:flagchain} yields the two complementary bounds
\begin{align}
d_r&\ge 6^r/\gamma_r^r,\label{eq:lowerwindow}\\
d_{r-1}&\le\gamma_r d_r^{(r-1)/r}.
\label{eq:upperwindow}
\end{align}
The first prevents a lattice of minimum six from having too small a
determinant. The second prevents its shortest-dual section from having too
large a determinant. Their interaction makes a search beginning in rank
one possible.

\section{Determinant congruences and the exact flag windows}
\label{sec:windows}

\subsection{The congruence modulo four}
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:mod4}
If \(G\) is a symmetric integral \(r\times r\) matrix with even
diagonal, then its determinant is even for odd \(r\). If \(r=2m\),
\[
\det G\equiv0\text{ or }(-1)^m\pmod4.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Over \(\F_2\), the form defined by \(G\) is alternating, since its
diagonal vanishes. An alternating form has even rank: if it is nonzero,
choose vectors with pairing one, split off their nonsingular
two-dimensional plane, and continue on the orthogonal complement.
Thus in odd dimension the determinant is zero modulo two.

Now let \(r=2m\). If \(G\bmod2\) is singular, its nullity is an even
positive number, hence at least two. At least two of its Smith invariant
factors are even, so \(4\mid\det G\). Otherwise an odd off-diagonal
entry gives an invertible block over \(\Z/4\Z\) of the form
\[
P=\begin{pmatrix}2a&b\\b&2c\end{pmatrix},\qquad b\text{ odd}.
\]
Its determinant is \(4ac-b^2\equiv-1\pmod4\). Orthogonal block
elimination over \(\Z/4\Z\) splits off this block: invertibility of
\(P\) allows the cross block to be killed by a triangular congruence
matrix. Congruence preserves the property of having even diagonal. The
remaining block is nonsingular modulo two and the process repeats.
There are \(m\) blocks. A change of basis multiplies the determinant by
the square of a unit modulo four, which equals one, so the product is
\((-1)^m\pmod4\).
\end{proof}

In particular, a hypothetical rank-nine determinant strictly below
\(21870\) satisfies
\begin{equation}
d_9\le21868.
\label{eq:topwindow}
\end{equation}
The even-rank restrictions alternate: ranks \(2,6\) allow residues
\(0,3\), while ranks \(4,8\) allow residues \(0,1\).

\subsection{The exceptional rank-eight equality case}
The Hermite lower bound gives \(d_8\ge6^8/256=6561=3^8\). Equality
forces \(L_8\cong\sqrt3E_8\) by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hermiteexternal}. We exclude it within a hypothetical
counterexample before raising the lower end of the rank-eight window.

A standard model of \(E_8\) is
\[
D_8\ \cup\ \bigl((\tfrac12,\ldots,\tfrac12)+D_8\bigr),
\qquad
D_8=\{x\in\Z^8:\textstyle\sum x_i\text{ is even}\}.
\]
This is a lattice because twice the half-vector belongs to \(D_8\).
It is integral and even; a direct calculation verifies the pairings and
parity in both cosets. Since \(D_8\) has index two in \(\Z^8\), it has
determinant four. The displayed union has index two over \(D_8\), so
\(E_8\) has determinant one by \eqref{eq:index}. Its minimum is two:
nonzero integral vectors of even coordinate sum have norm at least two,
half-integral vectors have norm at least \(8/4=2\), and this bound is
attained. Thus \(E_8\) is even unimodular and \(E_8^*=E_8\).

For \(J=\sqrt3E_8\), dual classes can be written as
\[

\]
There are \(3^8=6561\) classes. Exact enumeration gives the following
distribution of minimum \emph{unscaled} norms in \(E_8/3E_8\):

\end{center}

\]
Consequently a rank-nine continuation has determinant at least

This contradicts \eqref{eq:topwindow}. The next integer above \(6561\)
allowed by Lemma~\ref{lem:mod4} is \(6564\).

\section{The dual-vector lifting inequality}
\label{sec:lifting}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:lifting}
Let \(J\subset K\) be a saturated corank-one inclusion for which the
normal to \(J\) is shortest in \(K^*\). If
\(s=\det K/\det J\) and \(\lambda=\min J^*\), then
\[
s\ge\frac{3}{4\lambda}.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write \(K=J+\Z v\), and let \(y_0\in K^*\) be the perpendicular
normal with \(\langle y_0,v\rangle=1\). Then \(\|y_0\|^2=1/s\).
Choose \(u\in J^*\) with \(\|u\|^2=\lambda\). Rounding
\(\langle u,v\rangle\) to a nearest integer gives
\(c\in[-1/2,1/2]\) with
\(\langle u,v\rangle+c\in\Z\). Hence \(u+cy_0\in K^*\).
This vector is nonzero because its projection \(u\) to \(J\)'s span
is nonzero. Orthogonality gives
\[
\|u+cy_0\|^2=\lambda+c^2/s\le\lambda+1/(4s).
\]
Since \(y_0\) is shortest in \(K^*\),
\(1/s\le\lambda+1/(4s)\). Rearranging proves the claim.
\end{proof}

The constant \(1/4\) comes from the squared maximum distance to the
nearest integer. It is a rigorous universal rounding bound; no
probabilistic argument is involved. In the consecutive flag steps
\(L_6\subset L_7\subset L_8\), the previous normal identity gives
\(\min L_7^*=d_6/d_7\). Thus
\begin{equation}
d_8\ge\frac{3d_7^2}{4d_6}.
\label{eq:pairbound}
\end{equation}
Any determinant pair for which the right side exceeds \(15908\) can be
discarded before enumerating rank-eight extensions. Equality at the upper
endpoint would not permit discarding the pair.

The same inequality is used as a parent-level optimization in the
bottom-up generator: if \(D_+/\det J<3/(4\min J^*)\), no child in
the window can have its normal shortest. The generator returns an empty
list together with the exact inequality, rather than relying on a
heuristic estimate for the dual minimum.

\section{How the exact finite computation works}
\label{sec:algorithms}

\subsection{Rational elimination and positive definiteness}
Every Gram matrix and pairing vector is integral. Inverses, Schur
complements, class minima, and recursion bounds are represented by reduced
rational numbers. An exact \(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) decomposition writes
\[
A=L\diag(d_1,\ldots,d_r)L^{\mathsf T},
\]
where \(L\) is unit lower triangular. Recursively,
\begin{align*}
d_k&=A_{kk}-\sum_{h<k}L_{kh}^2d_h,\\
L_{ik}&=\frac{A_{ik}-\sum_{h<k}L_{ih}L_{kh}d_h}{d_k}
\quad(i>k).
\end{align*}
All \(d_k>0\) certify positive definiteness, since this is a congruence
to a positive diagonal matrix. Reconstruction of \(A\) can be checked
entry by entry. The same formulas apply to a positive-definite rational
matrix such as \(G^{-1}\).

Bareiss elimination computes integer determinants while controlling
denominator growth. If the current pivot is \(p\) and the previous
pivot is \(p_{\mathrm{old}}\), the trailing entries are updated by
\[
a_{ij}\longleftarrow
\frac{p\,a_{ij}-a_{ik}a_{kj}}{p_{\mathrm{old}}}.
\]
The initial previous pivot is one; the divisions are exact by the
determinantal identities underlying fraction-free elimination. Row swaps
are accompanied by a sign change. An exact implementation should verify
divisibility instead of treating integer truncation as a proof. In the
audited positive-definite Gram matrices all leading pivots are nonzero.
Unimodular isometry matrices need row pivoting and an appropriate sign
check. The supplementary audit also compared determinants with an
independent permutation formula on small cases and on the displayed
rank-nine matrix.

\subsection{Exact ellipsoid enumeration and integer endpoints}
The decomposition gives
\begin{equation}
x^{\mathsf T}Ax=
\sum_{k=1}^r d_k\left(x_k+\sum_{j>k}L_{jk}x_j\right)^2.
\label{eq:ldlenumeration}
\end{equation}
Enumerate coordinates from \(x_r\) down to \(x_1\). Suppose the higher
coordinates are fixed and have used norm \(U\le B\). At level \(k\),
put
\[
c=-\sum_{j>k}L_{jk}x_j,\qquad R=(B-U)/d_k.
\]
The allowed coordinates satisfy \((x_k-c)^2\le R\). Write
\(c=a/b\), \(b>0\), and \(R=p/q\ge0\), \(q>0\). Then
\[
(bx_k-a)^2\le b^2p/q.
\]
The left side is an integer square. Define
\[
N=\left\lfloor\sqrt{\left\lfloor b^2p/q\right\rfloor}\right\rfloor.
\]
The exact coordinate interval is
\begin{equation}
\left\lceil\frac{a-N}{b}\right\rceil
\le x_k\le
\left\lfloor\frac{a+N}{b}\right\rfloor.
\label{eq:exactinterval}
\end{equation}
All operations use integer division and integer square root. Flooring
before taking the integer square root is exact here because the quantity
being bounded is itself an integer square. Another audited enumerator
uses a slightly larger interval from a rational square-root ceiling and
then an exact membership test; that method is also complete.

To prove exhaustiveness, take any \(x\) with
\(x^{\mathsf T}Ax\le B\). Each partial sum in
\eqref{eq:ldlenumeration} is nonnegative and at most \(B\). At each
recursion level its coordinate therefore lies in
\eqref{eq:exactinterval}. Induction down the coordinates shows that the
algorithm visits \(x\). Conversely, every emitted vector satisfies the
exact sum bound. This proves both directions, including vectors exactly
on the boundary. Floating-point rounding cannot delete a boundary vector,
because it is never used in a membership decision.

\subsection{Isometries and the safe role of invariants}
To test whether \(A\) is isometric to \(R\), an exhaustive method
enumerates all target vectors of the prescribed basis norms \(A_{ii}\),
then chooses columns \(x_i\) satisfying
\[
x_i^{\mathsf T}Rx_j=A_{ij}\quad\text{for all }i,j.
\]
Each pool is finite and can be generated by the exact ellipsoid routine.
At a completed assignment, verify \(|\det X|=1\). This is a complete
search for a basis isometry.

Short-vector shell counts and pairing histograms are useful filters:
different invariants can rule out isometry, while equal invariants only
trigger the full test. The shell bound must be common to the forms being
compared; a basis-dependent cutoff is not an isometry invariant. Most
importantly, every \emph{positive merger} is certified by an explicit
unimodular matrix. A failure to merge two isometric forms only increases
work. An unsupported merger can delete a necessary branch and would
invalidate the proof. The archived maps make all positive mergers directly
checkable independently of the search heuristic used to find them.

\subsection{The induction proving completeness after deduplication}
\label{subsec:completeness}
Let \(\mathcal R_r\) be the retained representatives in rank \(r\).
The induction invariant is:
\begin{quote}
Every rank-\(r\) lattice occurring in a hypothetical flag satisfying
\eqref{eq:windows} is isometric to a representative in \(\mathcal R_r\).
\end{quote}
For \(r=1\), the window forces \([6]\), so the invariant holds.
Assume it holds for rank \(r\), and consider an actual flag inclusion
\(L_r\subset L_{r+1}\). Transfer it along an isometry of \(L_r\)
to its retained representative \(J\). Saturation writes the child as a
one-vector extension of \(J\). Translation of its generator by \(J\)
makes its projection a shortest representative of a discriminant class.
That class occurs in the complete class table, and its even last diagonal
entry lies in the enumerated interval. The layer and shortest-normal tests
accept the transferred child, because those are isometry-invariant
conditions that the actual flag satisfies. Deduplication then sends it to
a retained representative by an explicit integral isometry. This proves
the invariant in rank \(r+1\).

Deduplication need not remember the entire earlier flag. An isometry of
\(L_r\) transports that flag, so the necessary existence of an earlier
flag is preserved. The later extension tests use only the current parent
lattice and its actual dual minimum; they do not require a fixed basis
embedding of \(L_{r-1}\) into the representative. At the rank-seven
pruning stage the recorded determinant pair supplies
\(d_6/d_7=\min L_7^*\), an invariant of \(L_7\). This explains why
forgetting earlier labelled embeddings cannot remove a valid
counterexample.

\section{The equality witness and a small independent sharpness proof}
\label{sec:k9}

The source manuscript provides the symmetric matrix
\begin{equation}
K_9=\begin{pmatrix}
6&-3&-3&-3&-3&-3&-3&3&-2\\
-3&6&3&3&3&3&3&0&1\\
-3&3&6&0&3&0&0&-3&3\\

-3&3&3&3&6&3&3&-3&1\\

-3&3&0&3&3&3&6&0&1\\
3&0&-3&0&-3&0&0&6&-1\\
-2&1&3&-1&1&0&1&-1&6
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:K9}
\end{equation}
It is integral and even by inspection. The archived verifier checks
positive definiteness, determinant \(21870\), absence of nonzero
vectors of norm below six, and the presence of \(258\) oriented
minimum vectors. These checks were rerun in the current audit.

There is also a much smaller argument for its minimum, reducing the
remaining check to \(64\) explicitly bounded integer tuples. This
argument was reconstructed during the audit and does not rely on the
archived dual-ellipsoid enumerator.

Let \(G\) be the leading \(8\times8\) block of \(K_9\), set
\(C=G/3\), and let
\[
p=(-2,1,3,-1,1,0,1,-1)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Exact elementary matrix calculations give
\begin{gather}
\det C=1,\qquad C>0,\qquad
\diag(C^{-1})=(4,2,4,2,4,2,2,2),\label{eq:Cfacts}\\
w=C^{-1}p=(-1,-1,2,-1,0,0,1,1)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad w^{\mathsf T}Cw=8.
\label{eq:wfacts}
\end{gather}
For instance \(C>0\) can be checked by its positive rational
\(LDL^{\mathsf T}\) pivots, a short matrix calculation. It has even
diagonal and is integral, so every nonzero integer vector has norm at
least two, and a basis vector attains two. Thus \(G=3C\) has minimum
six and determinant \(3^8=6561\).

The projection of the ninth basis vector has coordinates
\(G^{-1}p=w/3\), so its squared norm is
\[
p^{\mathsf T}G^{-1}p=8/3.
\]
Its Schur complement is
\begin{equation}
s=6-8/3=10/3.
\label{eq:K9schur}
\end{equation}
This proves positive definiteness of \(K_9\) and
\[
\det K_9=6561\cdot10/3=21870.
\]
To prove the minimum by the layer criterion, it remains only to show that
the projected discriminant class has minimum exactly \(8/3\).

After multiplying coordinates by three, this amounts to showing
\begin{equation}
\min_{r\in w+3\Z^8}r^{\mathsf T}Cr=8.
\label{eq:64target}
\end{equation}
For \(r=w+3x\), even integrality gives
\[
r^{\mathsf T}Cr-w^{\mathsf T}Cw
=6x^{\mathsf T}Cw+9x^{\mathsf T}Cx\in6\Z.
\]
Thus a norm smaller than eight in this residue class could only be two.
For a vector of norm two, Cauchy--Schwarz in the inner product defined
by \(C\) implies
\[
|r_i|^2
=|\langle C^{-1}e_i,r\rangle_C|^2
\le(C^{-1})_{ii}\,r^{\mathsf T}Cr
\le8.
\]
All coordinates are therefore integers of absolute value at most two.
Combining these bounds with \(r\equiv w\pmod3\) leaves
\[
r_1,r_2,r_3,r_4\in\{-1,2\},\quad r_5=r_6=0,
\quad r_7,r_8\in\{-2,1\}.
\]
There are \(2^6=64\) tuples. Direct evaluation of
\(r^{\mathsf T}Cr\), using integer additions and multiplications,
gives:
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{c|rrrrrrrrrrr}
norm&8&14&20&26&32&38&44&50&56&62&86\\\hline
count&5&9&14&10&8&5&4&4&1&3&1
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The counts add to \(64\), and norm two does not occur. Since \(w\)
itself has norm eight, \eqref{eq:64target} follows. A short direct
reproduction appears in Appendix~\ref{app:64}.

We have now proved \(\mu_G(a)=8/3\). The layers \(m=\pm1\) have
minimum
\[
10/3+8/3=6.
\]
Every layer with \(|m|\ge2\) has perpendicular norm at least
\(4\cdot10/3=40/3>6\). The parent layer already has minimum six.
Hence \(\min K_9=6\). This proves the upper-bound part of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} and explains structurally why the threshold
\(21870\) appears: it is \(\det(\sqrt3E_8)\) times the least
perpendicular squared length permitted by a deepest dual class.

The auxiliary count \(258\) is a useful independent diagnostic but is
not required to establish the minimum or the determinant optimum. The
\(64\)-tuple argument is not claimed to enumerate all vectors of norm
eight; its coordinate bounds were derived for a hypothetical norm-two
vector and are used only to rule that possibility out.

\section{Orthogonal complements in unimodular lattices and packing density}
\label{sec:packing}

\subsection{The determinant identity for a primitive section}
Let \(\Lambda\) be integral unimodular, meaning
\(\det\Lambda=1\), equivalently \(\Lambda^*=\Lambda\). Let
\(K\subset\Lambda\) be saturated, and define
\[
L=\Lambda\cap(\Span_{\R}K)^\perp.
\]
Then \(L\) is saturated as well: if \(nx\) is orthogonal to \(K\)
for a nonzero integer \(n\), so is \(x\). We prove
\begin{equation}
\det L=\det K.
\label{eq:ambientdet}
\end{equation}

Because \(K\) is primitive as an abelian subgroup, a basis of it extends
to a basis of \(\Lambda\). Any integer-valued linear functional on
\(K\) therefore extends to one on \(\Lambda\). Unimodularity
identifies such functionals with pairings against elements of
\(\Lambda\). Consequently the restriction map
\[
\Lambda\longrightarrow K^*,\qquad
x\longmapsto\bigl(k\mapsto\langle x,k\rangle\bigr)
\]
is onto, and its kernel is \(L\). Modding out the image of \(K\)
gives
\[
\Lambda/(K\oplus L)\cong K^*/K.
\]
Put \(h=[\Lambda:K\oplus L]\). Then \(h=\det K\). By
orthogonality and the index-square formula,
\[
\det K\det L=\det(K\oplus L)=h^2\det\Lambda=h^2.
\]
Substituting \(h=\det K\) proves \eqref{eq:ambientdet}.

This derivation also pinpoints why unimodularity and primitivity cannot
be suppressed from the statement. For a nonsaturated section, the quotient
has extra torsion; for a nonunimodular ambient lattice, the restriction
map need not be onto in the same way.

\subsection{What density the determinant controls}
Suppose \(\Lambda\) has rank \(48\), is even unimodular, and has
minimum six. A primitive rank-nine section \(K\) and its orthogonal
complement \(L\) have ranks nine and \(39\). Both inherit minimum at
least six. Put balls of radius
\[
r_0=\sqrt6/2=\sqrt{3/2}
\]
around points of \(L\). Their interiors are disjoint. The center density,
which omits the volume of the unit ball, is
\[
\delta_{\mathrm{bare}}(L)=\frac{r_0^{39}}{\covol L}
=\frac{(3/2)^{39/2}}{\sqrt{\det K}}.
\]
Ordinary packing density is this quantity multiplied by
\(\vol(B_{39}(1))\). Theorem~\ref{thm:main} implies
\begin{equation}
\delta_{\mathrm{bare}}(L)
\le\frac{(3/2)^{39/2}}{\sqrt{21870}}
=\frac{3^{16}}{2^{20}\sqrt5}.
\label{eq:density}
\end{equation}
The last equality follows from \(21870=2\cdot3^7\cdot5\).

This proves an upper bound for the \emph{inherited-radius bare
cross-section construction}. The source reports a saturated ambient
embedding of its \(K_9\) construction, which would attain this density
at the inherited radius. The present audit verifies the abstract matrix
and the determinant obstruction; it does not rerun the separate ambient
coordinate embedding certificates of that source. Accordingly, the
attainment assertion for a specified ambient lattice remains attributed
to the supplied manuscript.

If an orthogonal complement happened to have minimum greater than six,
one could ask about increasing its packing radius; the fixed-radius
formula above does not by itself exclude every gain of that different
kind. Nor does it exclude antipode clusters, nonlinear packings, or
constructions unrelated to rank-48 sections. The exact conclusion is that
one cannot improve the source's dimension-39 bare construction by lowering
the rank-nine determinant within the stated even integral setting.

\subsection{The trust structure of the computer-assisted theorem}
It is useful to distinguish four logically different tasks.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Arithmetic validity.} A stored matrix has the stated
determinant and minimum; a stored map is unimodular and satisfies its
Gram identity; a recorded vector has its asserted norm.
\item \textbf{Coverage of finite searches.} Every needed parent, class,
even diagonal, layer, and short dual vector was considered. This requires
the exhaustive enumeration proof and code, not merely checking the
outputs one at a time.
\item \textbf{Mathematical reduction.} Every hypothetical counterexample
has a flag within the windows, and every retained branch implies the
required determinant floor. This is the structural proof in the earlier
sections.
\item \textbf{Scientific review.} External verification of the complete
argument, attribution and novelty, and suitability for publication are
additional tasks. An internal replay is not an external referee report
or a formal proof in a proof assistant.
\end{enumerate}
The present audit provides new work on the first three tasks, with the
reported boundary between full same-code regeneration and independent
checks. It does not claim to have completed the fourth.

room for cluster improvements, including above bare-optimal sections.\\[3pt]
Bare statements in dimensions 40--43, ambient constructions, and kissing
numbers elsewhere in the source&
Quoted for context; the present audit does not constitute a replay or a

\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

The final scope is deliberately tied to the universal statement actually
proved. A future equality classification must include determinant
\(21870\), retain all equality possibilities in the inequalities, and
check their extensions rather than applying the strict bound
\(d_9\le21868\). A future rank-ten argument requires its own
top-level determinant and Hermite analysis. A future cluster argument
must also track inter-coset distances and cluster cardinality; a section
determinant alone does not determine its density.

\section{A compact independent verification of the 64-tuple argument}
\label{app:64}

The following code uses only integer arithmetic and directly forms the
matrix \(C\) appearing in Section~\ref{sec:k9}. It verifies the finite
part of \eqref{eq:64target}; the preceding proof explains why checking
these tuples is sufficient.
\begin{verbatim}
from itertools import product
from collections import Counter

C = [
 [ 2,-1,-1,-1,-1,-1,-1, 1],
 [-1, 2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 0],
 [-1, 1, 2, 0, 1, 0, 0,-1],
 [-1, 1, 0, 2, 1, 1, 1, 0],
 [-1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 1, 1,-1],
 [-1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 2, 1, 0],
 [-1, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 0],
 [ 1, 0,-1, 0,-1, 0, 0, 2],
]
w = (-1,-1,2,-1,0,0,1,1)
choices = [(-1,2)]*4 + [(0,)]*2 + [(-2,1)]*2

def q(x):
    return sum(x[i]*C[i][j]*x[j]
               for i in range(8) for j in range(8))

counts = Counter(q(r) for r in product(*choices))
assert sum(counts.values()) == 64
assert counts == {
    8:5, 14:9, 20:14, 26:10, 32:8, 38:5,
    44:4, 50:4, 56:1, 62:3, 86:1,
}
assert q(w) == 8 and 2 not in counts
print("K9_RESIDUE_CLASS_MINIMUM_EIGHT")
\end{verbatim}
The supplementary verifier additionally checks \(C^{-1}\), positive
definiteness, determinant, the exact window endpoints, and the Schur
complement using independently written rational arithmetic.
